## Purpose

This comparative board traces how the eight-pointed star form travels across cultures, periods, and media — not as a borrowed symbol, but as a recurring response to a shared geometric problem: how to fill a circular field with rotational symmetry using whole-number divisions.

The board is NOT a diffusion argument. It is a structural comparison: the same construction logic (circle → eight-point division → star polygon) produces visually related forms independently and through contact.

---

## Concept 1: Mesopotamian Rosette Star

**Culture:** Mesopotamia, ancient Near East (3000–500 BCE)
**Form:** Eight-petal rosette, sometimes read as star, sometimes as flower
**Media:** Cylinder seal reliefs, textile borders, architectural ornament, Ishtar iconography
**Construction logic:** Radial petal division of a circle; no explicit polygon — the star emerges from petal overlaps
**Key formal property:** Curved petals, not pointed star arms. The geometry is organic-radial, not orthogonal-angular.
**Distinction from Islamic star:** The Mesopotamian rosette does not emphasize the negative space between petals as a distinct geometric shape. The Islamic star foregrounds the polygon lattice and its voids.

## Concept 2: Islamic Eight-Point Star (6/8/12 Family)

**Culture:** Islamic world, 9th–16th century CE (and continuing)
**Form:** Precise octagram, tessellating with cross/rhombus fill tiles
**Media:** Tilework (zellij, mosaic), carved stone and plaster (stucco), manuscript borders, metalwork
**Construction logic:** Compass construction — circle, octagonal division, alternating vertex connections forming two overlapping squares
**Key formal property:** The negative space (the cross/lozenge tiles between stars) is as geometrically precise as the star itself. The tile set is a complete interlocking system.
**Distinction from Mesopotamian rosette:** Angular, polygonal, based on straight-line construction. The star is defined by its exact polygon, not its organic petal count.

**Source query seeds:**

## Concept 3: North African Zellij

**Culture:** Maghreb (Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria), 10th century CE onward
**Form:** Cut-tile star-and-polygon mosaic
**Media:** Fired ceramic tiles, hand-cut (zellige technique), set in geometric patterns on walls and floors
**Construction logic:** Same polygon basis as Islamic star work, but executed as physical cut-tile assemblage rather than drawn or carved. Each tile shape is hand-cut to exact polygon specifications.
**Key formal property:** Color is part of the grammar — tile colors are assigned to polygon types (star tiles in one color, cross tiles in another, corner pieces in a third). The coloring reveals the tiling structure.
**Distinction:** The material process (cut fired clay) introduces slight irregularities that distinguish zellij from print-perfect geometric diagrams. The handcraft variation is a quality, not an error.

## Concept 4: Renaissance Rose Window Afterlife

**Culture:** Europe, Gothic into Renaissance, 12th–16th century CE
**Form:** Circular window divided by stone tracery into radiating petal/star units
**Media:** Gothic cathedral architecture; oculus windows; later Renaissance circular window designs
**Construction logic:** Compass geometry — circle divided by inscribed polygon (usually 8, 12, or 16 divisions); lancet arcs form the petal shapes between radial divisions
**Key formal property:** The form is architectural and transparent (glass/opening), not decorative and opaque. The star/rosette form admits light, making the geometry a light-modulating device.
**Distinction:** The Gothic rose window is simultaneously structural (the stone tracery must bear load) and geometric. Renaissance interest in it is antiquarian — the form is preserved as visual reference.

## Learning design notes

- This board is designed for students who can already draw the Islamic 8-star construction (`flat-construction-vector` prerequisite).
- The Mesopotamian rosette comparison should precede the Islamic star — it establishes that "eight" and "radial" are separate from "angular star".
- The rose window comparison should follow the Islamic star — it shows the same form logic applied in a different structural and material context.
- Do not present this as "Islam influenced Renaissance." Present it as "both drew on the same geometric logic."
