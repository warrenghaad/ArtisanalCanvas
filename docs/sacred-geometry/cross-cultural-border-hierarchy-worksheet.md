## Purpose

Every tradition that uses geometric pattern also solves the border problem: how does a continuous pattern stop? How does a flat surface announce its edge? How does a drawing know where it ends?

The border is not an afterthought — it is a full geometric sub-problem. This board makes the structural logic of borders comparable across six traditions.

---

## The four structural levels of a border system

Most rich border systems operate at 4 hierarchical levels:

1. **Inner guard band** — thin, simple, immediately adjacent to the central field. Often a single line or minimal stripe.
2. **Secondary band** — a distinct, narrower geometric register. Creates breathing room between field and main border.
3. **Main border register** — the primary ornamental border. Usually the widest and most complex geometric band.
4. **Outer guard band** — the outermost framing element. Often mirrors the inner guard band.

Not every tradition uses all four levels. The comparison shows which levels each tradition uses and what geometric vocabulary fills each level.

## Tradition 1: Persian Manuscript Border

**Period:** 15th–17th century CE (Safavid and earlier)
**Media:** Ink, pigment, and gold leaf on paper
**Border system structure:**
- Inner guard: gold ruled line (1–2mm)
- Secondary band: narrow arabesque or cloud-collar interlace
- Main border: full islimi (arabesque vine scroll) with cartouche interruptions
- Outer guard: blue ruled line with occasional gold dots

**Key geometric feature:** The islimi main border is a continuous vine scroll that maintains the illusion of an unbroken line while accommodating complex corner turns. The corner turn logic is a separate geometric problem — the vine must bend 90° without breaking the scrolling rhythm.

**Border width ratio:** Inner guard : secondary : main : outer ≈ 1 : 3 : 12 : 1

**Source query seeds:**
- Safavid manuscript border illumination gold arabesque
- Persian Quran illumination border hierarchy
- Timurid manuscript border islimi scroll corner

## Tradition 2: Greek Pottery Border

**Period:** 700–400 BCE (Archaic and Classical periods)
**Media:** Slip painting on ceramic vessels
**Border system structure:**
- Multiple register bands, typically 3–5 narrow horizontal bands
- Each band uses a different simple pattern: meander, wave, dot-row, cable, egg-and-dart
- The meander band is the most architecturally prominent (widest)
- No outer/inner guard distinction — all bands are guard-scale, stacked

**Key geometric feature:** The register system is additive — more bands = more visual weight. The meander band anchors the composition as a horizontal axis. All other bands are secondary.

**Border width ratio:** Typically 1:1:2:1:1 for 5-band systems (meander occupying the widest band)

**Source query seeds:**
- Greek black figure vase border register bands
- Greek pottery meander key pattern border Attic
- Greek geometric period pottery border registers

## Tradition 3: Roman Mosaic Border

**Period:** 1st century BCE – 4th century CE
**Media:** Stone, glass, and ceramic tesserae
**Border system structure:**
- Outer guard: simple black-and-white checker row or plain stripe
- Secondary band: guilloché (continuous wave/braid), perspective cube pattern, or dentil row
- Main border: guilloché, egg-and-dart, or meander (Greek key revival)
- Inner guard: plain stripe, often 2–3 tesserae wide

**Key geometric feature:** Roman mosaic border logic is architectural — it mirrors the entablature of a building (outer cornice → frieze → inner molding). The tesserae scale means the border resolution is fixed by material grain.

**Border width ratio:** Outer : secondary : main : inner ≈ 2 : 5 : 10 : 3

**Source query seeds:**
- Roman mosaic floor border guilloché meander museum
- Roman mosaic tesserae border hierarchy checkerboard
- Pompeii mosaic border register

## Tradition 4: Islamic Manuscript Border

**Period:** 9th–16th century CE (Abbasid through Ottoman)
**Media:** Ink and pigment on paper, often with gold
**Border system structure:**
- Inner guard: gold or blue ruled line
- Secondary band: geometric interlace or kufic inscription
- Main border: geometric star-polygon lattice or full arabesque
- Outer guard: ruled lines with margin design

**Key geometric feature:** The geometric interlace main border uses the same star-polygon construction logic as architectural tilework but scaled to manuscript proportions. The corner solution for geometric interlace (where two bands of interlace must meet at 90°) is more complex than for organic scrolls — it requires a specific geometric patch.

**Border width ratio:** Inner : secondary : main : outer ≈ 1 : 4 : 10 : 1

**Source query seeds:**
- Islamic Quran manuscript border geometric interlace
- Mamluk manuscript illumination border star polygon
- Ottoman manuscript border corner solution geometric

## Tradition 5: African Textile Borders (selected)

**Traditions included:** Kente, Adinkra, Mudcloth, Kuba

**Common border structures:**

| Textile | Inner guard | Secondary | Main | Outer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kente | Color stripe | — | Patterned strip band | Color stripe |
| Adinkra | Ink grid line | — | Symbol row | Ink grid line |
| Mudcloth | Chevron band | Triangle row | Central field (inverse of border) | Chevron band |
| Kuba | Plain woven strip | Narrow geometric band | Complex broken-grid field | Plain strip |

**Key geometric feature:** African textile borders are typically generated by the same process as the field — they are not a separate ornamental system but a different application of the same stamp, weave unit, or mark. The border IS the grid's edge condition.

**Source query seeds:**
- Kente cloth selvage border stripe
- Adinkra cloth edge border ink grid
- Mudcloth border chevron geometry Mali
- Kuba raffia border plain strip geometry

## Cross-tradition structural comparison

| Property | Persian MS | Greek Pottery | Roman Mosaic | Islamic MS | African Textile |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of border levels | 4 | 3–5 (all guard-scale) | 4 | 4 | 2–3 |
| Dominant border element | Islimi scroll | Meander | Guilloché | Star interlace | Stripe / chevron |
| Corner solution strategy | Scroll turn | Register wraps | Geometric patch | Geometric patch | None (borders end) |
| Width ratio main:guard | ~12:1 | ~2:1 | ~5:1 | ~10:1 | ~4:1 |
| Material constraint | Paper | Ceramic slip | Tesserae grain | Paper | Woven/stamped unit |

## Student exercise (worksheet)

1. Find a rectangular frame (or draw one, 20×30cm).
2. Draw a 4-level border system in the style of **one** tradition from this board.
3. For the corner turn: sketch how your chosen border element bends at 90°.
4. Mark the width ratio of your four border levels.
5. Compare your corner solution with a partner using a different tradition.

**Discussion question:** Which tradition makes the corner turn hardest? Which makes it easiest? Why?
